FareOrbit

Flight search that admits what it doesn’t know.

Every price has an age.

FareOrbit searches the flight data it can legitimately reach, says how old every price is and what it did not search, and hands you to the provider to book. It is being built in the open by one person.

In development · not yet released

Read this before anything else

FareOrbit is not finished and you cannot use it yet. There is no public app, nothing to download, and no booking of any kind.

The fares are now real, but thin. They come from one provider’s cache, which returns a single price per route and date — and nothing at all for many routes — and that price may be up to seven days old. FareOrbit says so on every fare. It cannot compare the market today, and it does not claim to.

What it is meant to do

Search flights across the providers it can reach, compare what comes back, and let you track a route so you hear about it when the price moves. When you choose a fare, FareOrbit hands you to the provider that sells it. Comparison is the goal; one provider is where it currently starts.

FareOrbit does not sell tickets, take payments, or hold your card details. It is a comparison tool, not a travel agency.

Where it actually is today

An honest status board rather than a roadmap.

  • WORKING Search, filtering and fare detail. The backend and the Android app are built and tested, and the search journey runs end to end.
  • WORKING Saved searches and price alerts. Including the dull but important parts: a failed price check is never recorded as a price change.
  • WORKING Real fares, from one provider. Live since 18 October 2026, via Aviasales’ cached price data.
  • THIN Comparing them. That provider returns one price per route and date, and none at all for many routes, so there is usually nothing to compare it against. A second source is the next thing that changes this.
  • NOT STARTED Released to anyone. The app is not on Google Play and has no public build.
  • NOT STARTED Explore by budget. Deliberately deferred until the core search is genuinely finished.

The rules it is built to

These are enforced in code and pinned by tests, not aspirations on a page.

Unknown stays unknown

If a provider doesn’t say whether a bag is included, FareOrbit says “Unknown”. It never rounds a gap up into a benefit.

It says what it searched

Every result states which providers answered and which failed. No claim to search every airline, because that would not be true.

A price has an age

Where a fare is cached rather than live, it says so and says how old it might be — not when the server last made a call.

No invented links

If a provider gives no link to a specific fare, FareOrbit says so. It will not quietly send you to an airline’s homepage instead.

Why be this blunt?

Fare comparison is a field with a lot of confident-sounding claims in it: cheapest anywhere, every airline, prices updated live. Most of them are difficult to substantiate, and a traveller has no way to check.

FareOrbit is being built the other way round — state the coverage, show the age of the data, and admit the gaps. That is less exciting on a landing page. It is considerably more useful when you are about to spend several hundred pounds on a flight.

Follow along

There is no mailing list and no launch date, because neither would be honest yet.

“FareOrbit” is a working project name. No trademark search, domain registration or app-store listing has been completed, and the name may change.

When FareOrbit does link to a booking provider, it may earn a commission on a completed booking. It will say so where it happens, and a commission will never change the order fares are shown in.
